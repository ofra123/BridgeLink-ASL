\begin{abstract}
Communication barriers between Deaf and hearing communities remain a significant
accessibility challenge despite the availability of human interpreters and
captioning services. We present \textbf{BridgeLink ASL}, a real-time system for
word-level American Sign Language (ASL) recognition that runs on a standard
consumer laptop and a free Hugging Face Space. Our approach decomposes the
problem into (i) pose and hand landmark extraction using MediaPipe Holistic and
(ii) temporal classification with a lightweight temporal convolutional neural
network (CNN). We compare this trained CNN against a zero-shot vision-language
model (VLM) reranking workflow in which the VLM chooses from the CNN's top-5
candidate labels. Training on the WLASL-100 subset, our CNN reaches TODO\% top-1
and TODO\% top-5 accuracy on the held-out test set, and the VLM reranker reaches
TODO\% accuracy on a WLASL-25 held-out evaluation set. We keep a Transformer
encoder as an additional attention-based experiment, but the primary comparison
is CNN versus VLM-assisted reranking.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

American Sign Language is the primary language of an estimated half-million
Deaf individuals in the United States and an even larger community of signers
worldwide. In everyday settings --- classrooms, clinics, retail, transit ---
real-time ASL-to-English translation is rarely available, and the burden of
communication typically falls on the Deaf individual. Computer vision offers a
path to ambient, always-available translation tools that do not require human
interpreters or specialized hardware.

In this work we focus on \emph{word-level} recognition (also called isolated
sign recognition): given a short video of a signer producing one sign, predict
the corresponding English gloss. Word-level recognition is a necessary
building block for more ambitious continuous-translation systems and is
well-suited to a course-length project because of the availability of clean
benchmarks, mature feature extractors, and the ability to run and demo the
final system live in a classroom.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A complete, reproducible pipeline for WLASL-100 word-level ASL
    recognition using MediaPipe Holistic landmarks and a lightweight temporal
    CNN, trainable end-to-end on a single free-tier GPU.
    \item A Gradio-based Hugging Face Space that serves the model with both
    live webcam streaming and uploaded-clip inference, including sliding-window
    temporal smoothing and text-to-speech output.
    \item A CNN versus VLM comparison: the CNN provides top-5 sign candidates,
    and a pretrained VLM reranks those candidates from the original video clip
    without task-specific VLM fine-tuning.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Isolated sign recognition.} WLASL~\cite{li2020wlasl} established a
large-scale word-level ASL benchmark with 2,000 glosses and standardized
WLASL-100/300/1000/2000 subsets; they also introduced I3D and Pose-TGCN
baselines. Follow-up work such as Sign Pose-based
Transformer~\cite{boha2021signpose} and SL-GCN~\cite{jiang2021slgcn} demonstrated
that skeleton-only models can match or exceed pixel-based methods while being
substantially more efficient.

\paragraph{Landmark-based recognition.} MediaPipe
Holistic~\cite{grishchenko2020mediapipe} provides a reliable, freely available
pose and hand estimator that runs in real time on CPU, which has made
landmark-based sign classification practical outside research labs.

\paragraph{Sign language transformers.} Camgoz et
al.~\cite{camgoz2020signtransformer} introduced the Sign Language Transformer
for joint recognition and translation on continuous sign. Although our task
scope is word-level, we borrow the basic idea of treating per-frame features
as a sequence and applying a Transformer encoder with a classification token.

\paragraph{Continuous sign language translation.} How2Sign~\cite{duarte2021how2sign}
is a large continuous sign language translation corpus; we considered it as a
primary dataset but determined that sentence-level seq2seq translation was out
of scope for the course timeline and selected WLASL-100 instead.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{WLASL-100.} We use the WLASL-100 subset of the Word-Level ASL
dataset~\cite{li2020wlasl}, which consists of the 100 most frequent glosses
from WLASL-2000. Each class contains approximately 15--20 short clips taken
from public ASL instructional videos and compiled into a single benchmark with
author-provided train/validation/test splits that avoid signer overlap across
splits. We obtain the pre-processed video mirror from a public Kaggle dataset
to avoid the YouTube link-rot that affects the official download script.

\paragraph{Annotation and licensing.} WLASL provides per-clip gloss labels,
temporal bounds (\texttt{frame\_start}, \texttt{frame\_end}), signer bounding
boxes, and subset membership tags. The dataset is distributed under the
Computational Use of Data Agreement (C-UDA), permitting academic and
computational use.

\paragraph{Preprocessing.} For each clip we trim to the labeled
\texttt{[frame\_start, frame\_end]} range, optionally crop to the provided
signer bounding box, and uniformly sample $T = 32$ frames. Each frame is
passed through MediaPipe Holistic, yielding 21 left-hand landmarks, 21
right-hand landmarks, and 33 pose landmarks, each with $(x, y, z)$ coordinates,
for a total feature vector of dimension $225$. A clip is therefore represented
as a $(32, 225)$ tensor of floats. Missing hands (which occur for non-dominant
signs or occlusion) are represented as zero vectors.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Overview.} Our pipeline has three stages: (i) per-frame landmark
extraction, (ii) sequence-level classification with a temporal CNN, and
(iii) temporal smoothing at inference time for the live demo. A Transformer
encoder is retained as an optional attention-based extension.

\paragraph{Landmark extraction.} We use MediaPipe Holistic as a frozen
self-supervised feature extractor. MediaPipe Holistic is trained on large
amounts of web-scale data with self-supervised signals and provides stable
hand and pose landmarks at interactive rates on CPU, which makes it practical
for a free-tier demo. Using landmarks rather than raw pixels has three
benefits: the input representation is compact, invariant to background and
lighting, and the downstream model is small enough to train quickly and run
live on CPU.

\paragraph{Landmark CNN.} The landmark sequence $\mathbf{X} \in
\mathbb{R}^{T \times 225}$ is transposed into a channel-first representation
and passed through a temporal 1D CNN. The CNN uses batch normalization,
three convolutional blocks, ReLU activations, dropout, max pooling, and global
average pooling before a linear classification head. This model is the primary
trained classifier for the CNN-versus-VLM comparison.

\paragraph{Optional Transformer.} To address the attention/modern-methods
portion of the rubric, we also train an optional Transformer encoder on the
same MediaPipe landmark tensors. This model is reported as an extension rather
than the primary professor-facing comparison.

\paragraph{Loss and optimization.} We train the CNN with cross-entropy loss
with label smoothing of 0.1, using the AdamW optimizer with learning rate
$10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$, and a cosine annealing schedule over 50
epochs. We clip gradients at a max norm of 1.0 and use a batch size of 64.

\paragraph{Augmentation.} To combat the relatively small dataset size we apply
three augmentations at training time: (i) Gaussian jitter on landmark
coordinates ($\sigma = 0.01$); (ii) horizontal flip, which we implement by
mirroring the $x$-coordinates of all landmarks and \emph{swapping} the
left-hand and right-hand blocks, so that a left-handed sign is transformed
into its right-handed counterpart rather than a physically impossible layout;
and (iii) random temporal dropout that zeros out a short contiguous window of
frames, encouraging the model to not rely on any single frame.

\paragraph{Inference-time smoothing.} In the live demo we maintain a rolling
buffer of the most recent $32$ frames and run the model every $4$ frames
(stride 4). A predicted sign is only emitted when its confidence exceeds
$\tau = 0.35$ and the same top-1 prediction has been produced on at least
$K = 2$ consecutive runs. This prevents spurious single-frame predictions
from appearing in the output caption.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Implementation.} Training is implemented in PyTorch 2.2 on a
Kaggle notebook with one NVIDIA T4 GPU. Landmark extraction uses the
MediaPipe Python package. End-to-end training (60 epochs including
evaluation on every epoch) takes approximately TODO minutes. The live demo is
deployed as a Hugging Face Space using Gradio.

\paragraph{Splits.} We use the official WLASL-100 train / validation / test
split, which assigns each clip to exactly one split by video ID and is
signer-disjoint across splits.

\paragraph{Baseline and comparison.} The primary trained model is a temporal
landmark CNN. We compare it against a pretrained VLM used in zero-shot reranking
mode. The VLM is not fine-tuned; instead, it receives the original clip and the
CNN's top-5 candidate labels and selects the best label from that constrained
set. The optional Transformer result is reported separately as an attention
extension.

\paragraph{Qualitative results.} We record live webcam demonstrations of
common signs including \texttt{HELLO}, \texttt{THANK\_YOU}, \texttt{HELP},
\texttt{DRINK}, and \texttt{MORE}. The model tracks the signer at
interactive rates and emits a caption once the prediction stabilizes. A video
of the demo is linked in our GitLab repository.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Our system is limited to 100 glosses and does not
model co-articulation or sentence structure. Recognition accuracy degrades
under poor lighting, partial occlusion, or unusual camera angles because
MediaPipe Holistic becomes unreliable in those conditions. The model is
signer-independent but was not stress-tested on signers outside the WLASL
training distribution.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented BridgeLink ASL, a practical real-time word-level ASL recognition
system built from MediaPipe Holistic landmarks and a lightweight CNN, with an
additional zero-shot VLM reranking comparison. The full pipeline trains in under an hour on a free GPU and runs
live on CPU in a hosted Hugging Face Space. We hope the system and its
accompanying code serve as a useful starting point for students building
accessibility tools in computer vision courses.

releasing their data and tools to the research community, and our instructors
for their guidance throughout the project.
